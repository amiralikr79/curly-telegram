**Body Copy:**

```
The Willow House explores how a four-unit multiplex can provide the 
privacy and spatial quality typically associated with single-family 
homes—while fitting within the urban constraints of a standard 
7,500 sqft lot.

The design organizes four family-sized units around a shared central 
courtyard—a semi-private outdoor space that provides natural light, 
cross-ventilation, and visual connection while maintaining acoustic 
and visual privacy between units.

Each unit is designed as a vertical home: main living areas on the 
entry level, bedrooms above, and flexible basement space for family 
rooms, home offices, or multi-generational living arrangements.

The material palette is deliberately restrained: vertical cedar siding 
weathering to silver-gray, cast-in-place concrete for the base and 
courtyard walls, and large-format windows with dark bronze aluminum 
frames.

Deep roof overhangs protect south-facing glass from summer sun while 
allowing low winter sun to penetrate interior spaces—a passive solar 
strategy rooted in West Coast climate responsiveness.

This is not a project about density maximization. It's about creating 
homes that feel generous, private, and grounded—despite their shared-lot 
configuration.
```

**Body Copy:**

```
The Ridgeview takes advantage of a sloped site to create six split-level 
homes that step down the hillside—each unit gaining panoramic views 
while maintaining privacy from neighbors above and below.

The project is designed around passive solar principles: south-facing 
glazing captures winter sun, while north-facing bedrooms remain cool 
in summer. Deep overhangs and exterior sunshades prevent overheating 
during warm months without blocking desirable winter light.

Each unit functions as a compact terrace home with its own private 
outdoor space—either a rooftop terrace or ground-level patio—eliminating 
the need for shared amenity spaces.

The material palette reflects the Pacific Northwest context: standing 
seam metal roofing in dark gray, fiber cement siding in warm white, 
and cedar accent cladding that will silver over time.

Structurally, the building is organized as three stacked duplexes, 
each with independent entries—creating the feel of attached townhomes 
rather than a monolithic apartment building.

This is a project about working with the land, not against it—using 
slope as an asset for views, light, and spatial differentiation rather 
than treating it as an obstacle to overcome.
```

```
The Larch Residences blend West Coast modernism with craftsman-era 
detail—creating three generously proportioned homes that feel rooted 
in their established residential context.

Unlike typical multiplex developments that maximize lot coverage, 
this project preserves significant green space: front yard setbacks 
with mature trees, side yards for natural light and fire separation, 
and rear yards for private outdoor living.

Each unit is designed as a true family home with 1,500–1,650 sqft 
across 2.5 floors—including main-floor living, upper-floor bedrooms, 
and a flexible loft space that can serve as a fourth bedroom, home 
office, or media room.

The material palette emphasizes warmth and craft: horizontal larch 
wood siding (a durable European softwood that ages to honey-gold), 
black-painted metal roofing, oversized wood-trimmed windows, and 
board-formed concrete foundation walls.

Front porches—deep enough for furniture—create a threshold between 
public street and private home, encouraging neighborly interaction 
while maintaining privacy.

Interior layouts prioritize multi-generational flexibility: main-floor 
bedrooms (accessible for aging family members), open-plan living for 
family gathering, and upper-floor bedroom suites with ensuite bathrooms.

This is a project about contextual integration—fitting into an 
established neighborhood while offering the spatial generosity and 
design quality that justifies gentle densification.
```
